The familiar four-circle “ikigai” diagram is a modern interpretation inspired by the Japanese idea of a life worth living. Treat it as a thinking tool, not a rigid formula.

Your four levers

Collect evidence,
not slogans.

Short lists are fine. Specific examples are better than impressive words. Everything you type below is saved automatically in your browser’s local storage.

01

What you
love

Think about activities that pull you in naturally — the subjects you return to, the problems you enjoy thinking about, and the work that leaves you energised rather than merely relieved.

  • What do you read, watch or learn about without being told?
  • What kind of work makes time disappear?
  • What did you enjoy before status or money mattered?
  • Which problems do you keep wanting to improve?
02

What you’re
good at

Separate proven strengths from things you merely enjoy. Look for abilities that other people notice, rely on, or ask you to help with.

  • What do people regularly ask you for help with?
  • What have you learned unusually quickly?
  • Where do you produce reliable results?
  • Which skills could become excellent with focused practice?
03

What the world
needs

“The world” can mean your town, an industry, a type of customer, a community or a very specific group of people. Useful work usually starts with a real problem for a real person.

  • Who do you understand well enough to genuinely help?
  • Which recurring frustrations seem unnecessarily difficult?
  • What change in technology or society creates a new need?
  • Which problems feel important enough to work on for years?
04

What you can
be paid for

This is the reality check. Who has a budget, what result do they value, and how could your contribution be packaged as employment, consulting, a service, a product or a business?

  • What outcomes already have money attached to them?
  • Who benefits enough to pay for the result?
  • Could this be a job, service, product, subscription or training?
  • What evidence do you have that demand exists?
The fifth step

Turn your notes into a serious career question.

Generate a structured prompt that asks an LLM to find occupations, business directions and experiments that sit closest to the overlap of your four answers.

Tip: the best result will usually come from answering with concrete examples, not just words such as “creative”, “people” or “technology”.
