Your four levers

Collect clues.
Build a direction.

Keep it light, specific and honest. A handful of concrete examples is more useful than a long list of vague adjectives. Everything you type below is stored automatically in your browser’s local storage.

01

What you
love

愛

Think about the activities, topics and kinds of work that naturally pull you in. What gives you energy? What do you keep coming back to, even when nobody is asking you to?

  • What do you read, watch or learn about for fun?
  • What kind of work makes time disappear?
  • What did you love before status or money became a factor?
  • Which problems do you genuinely enjoy thinking about?
02

What you’re
good at

技

Separate enjoyment from proven ability. Look for strengths that other people notice, rely on, or repeatedly ask you to help with.

  • What do people regularly ask you for help with?
  • What have you learned unusually quickly?
  • Where do you produce consistent, reliable results?
  • Which skills could become excellent with focused practice?
03

What the world
needs

世

“The world” may really mean a town, industry, customer group or community. Useful work usually begins with a real problem for a real person.

  • Who do you understand well enough to genuinely help?
  • Which recurring frustrations seem unnecessarily difficult?
  • What social or technological change is creating a new need?
  • Which problems feel important enough to work on for years?
04

What you can
be paid for

価

This is the reality check. Where is there already money, budget or demand? How could your contribution be packaged as a job, service, product, subscription or small business?

  • What outcomes already have money attached to them?
  • Who benefits enough to pay for the result?
  • Could this become a service, product, job, training or subscription?
  • What evidence suggests the demand is real?
The fifth step

Turn your notes into a strong AI prompt.

This final step gathers your four answers and turns them into a well-structured question for an LLM — asking it to suggest occupations, business directions and small experiments based on the overlap and tensions between your answers.

Tip: concrete examples usually produce better answers than general labels like “creative”, “techy” or “good with people”.
